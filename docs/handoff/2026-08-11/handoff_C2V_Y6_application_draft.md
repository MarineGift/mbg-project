### ▸ Summary of your solution (75 words 제한 / 74)

> We convert captured industrial CO₂ into flexible calcium carbonate, a papermaking filler grown in situ on cellulose fibrils. Conventional filler weakens paper above 25–35% loading; ours holds strength past 50%, letting mills replace wood pulp — which costs twice as much and carries 400–510 kg CO₂ per tonne. Each tonne avoids 220–330 kg CO₂ net. Validated at commercial mill scale with a 9,000-tonne order placed. No subsidy required: mills adopt because it is cheaper.

# SECTION 2: TECHNOLOGY (13문항)

### ▸ Q1. Fundamental operating principles (150 / 148)

> Paper is wood pulp plus mineral filler. Filler costs less than half what pulp costs, but above 25–35% loading the sheet loses strength, because mineral particles block hydrogen bonding between fibers. That ceiling has constrained papermaking for decades.
>
> We change the filler's architecture. Calcium oxide, ground calcium carbonate and unrefined pulp are milled together in water. The minerals act as grinding media, fibrillating the pulp to sub-micron width, while their hydration holds the slurry alkaline, which sharply lowers the energy fibrillation requires. Ionic polymers flocculate the mixture. Captured CO₂ is then injected at 10–80 °C until the slurry reaches pH 7.0, and calcium carbonate nucleates and grows directly on the fibril surfaces.
>
> The product is 80–99% mineral by volume with cellulose fibrils at its core. Those fibrils integrate into the paper's fiber network rather than interrupting it, so sheets hold strength beyond 50% filler.

### ▸ Q2. Tests, demonstrations, pilots, third-party validation (200 / 196)

> Development ran thirteen years, moving from laboratory work at a national university through pilot paper machines to commercial trials.
>
> The decisive validation was multi-year testing with a major pulp and paper producer, on that mill's own production machines rather than pilot equipment. Testing concluded in 2026. The mill then placed a firm order of 9,000 tonnes annually — the strongest validation available to us, since it is a purchase decision rather than a technical opinion.
>
> Production runs inside an existing commercial carbonate plant operated by a global top-three filler producer, adjacent to the mill. Neither party installed new reactors.
>
> Underlying performance is published and peer-reviewed (*ACS Sustainable Chemistry & Engineering* 2020, 8, 8994–9001; *ACS Omega* 2022). Against conventional ground calcium carbonate at identical 25% loading, our filler delivers 3.53 km breaking length versus 2.18, bulk of 1.88 versus 1.71 cm³/g, and bending stiffness of 636 versus 246 Gurley units, while holding brightness at 88.8%. At 57.5% ash — far beyond any conventional filler — our sheets still exceed a 33% ash conventional sheet on strength and bulk.
>
> A second global filler producer, US-headquartered, has signed an NDA and is running qualification testing.

### ▸ Q3. Scale-up roadmap (200 / 197)

> Current operating scale: 9,000 tonnes per year, ordered and entering supply. Production occupies an existing carbonate plant; we added no equipment.
>
> Our scale-up is not a plant-building exercise. Three deployment paths all add equipment to plants that already exist.
>
> **Path 1 (running).** A carbonate plant adjacent to a mill that operates its own microfibrillated cellulose unit. Both assets already existed.
>
> **Path 2 (12 months).** Any satellite carbonate plant can produce our filler by adding one fibril unit. Quantities are small — roughly 3 kg of fibril per tonne of product, so 10,000 tonnes needs 30 tonnes of fibril annually. The two producers we are engaged with operate several dozen plants between them; at 5,000 tonnes each, that is over 300,000 tonnes reachable inside existing infrastructure.
>
> **Path 3 (technical objective).** Moving production to ground-carbonate plants, which already own the grinding circuit our process needs. This halves lime consumption and delivers our best carbon performance. We hold laboratory data; commercial-scale validation is the milestone.
>
> Interim milestones: secure durable point-source CO₂ supply; validate Path 3 at plant scale; complete US qualification testing with our second producer; establish first US production.

### ▸ Q8. Pain point validated through market/customer discovery? (200 / 193)

> The pain point is not one we discovered — it is the defining economic constraint of the industry, and mills have been trying to solve it for decades.
>

That entire body of prior work is our market validation: the willingness to pay has been demonstrated repeatedly by the money spent trying.
>

### ▸ Q9. Unique / differentiating vs competitors and status quo (200 / 198)

> Growing calcium carbonate on cellulose in situ is not itself new. What is new is how we make the fibrils.
>
> Existing in-situ carbonation routes prepare nanocellulose first, as a separate upstream operation, then carbonate it. That preparation is the expensive part: beating pulp to 100 CSF, then thirty passes through a super mass colloider. Our comparative data put that at 185 kWh per tonne of beating plus 17 MWh per tonne of fibrillation.
>
> We eliminate that step by merging it into one that was already happening. Minerals and unrefined pulp enter the same mill. The minerals do double duty — grinding media that fibrillate the pulp by friction, and an alkali source that softens the fiber wall so fibrillation proceeds at a fraction of the usual energy. One hour of milling takes pulp from 15.5 µm to under 800 nm while reducing the mineral from 10 µm to roughly 3 µm. The fibril is never a product we buy or make; it is a byproduct of grinding mineral we needed to grind anyway.
>
> Against the status quo: conventional filler forces a trade between cost and strength. We remove the trade.

# SECTION 3: CLIMATE IMPACT (4문항 · 각 100 words)

### ▸ Q1. How does your innovation mitigate climate change? Durability? (100 / 99)

> Two effects, reported separately.
>
> **Utilization.** 431 kg of CO₂ is chemically fixed as carbonate per tonne of product. Our feedstock is captured industrial CO₂, not merchant gas.
>
> **Avoidance.** Each tonne of our filler displaces roughly a tonne of chemical pulp, whose cradle-to-gate footprint is 400–510 kg CO₂. Net avoidance is 220–330 kg CO₂ per tonne.
>
> On durability: carbonate in paper is thermodynamically stable and does not off-gas. Recovered fiber carries it into recycled grades; landfilled paper retains it indefinitely. Only incineration releases it. We do not claim permanent removal — we claim durable utilization plus substantial avoidance.

### ▸ Q2. Climate impact to date and at scale — methodology (100 / 100)

> To date: 9,000 tonnes ordered, representing 1,980–2,970 tCO₂ avoided annually. At the 300,000 tonnes reachable within our partners' existing plants, 66,000–99,000 tCO₂ annually.
>
> Methodology: mass balance on the carbonation reaction, plus attributional accounting of inputs. Key assumptions — 431 kg CO₂ fixed per tonne of product (stoichiometric, matched to our filed examples); lime carries roughly 1.0–1.2 tCO₂ per tonne upstream; fibril input is 3 kg per tonne of product, so fiber-preparation energy is negligible; displaced pulp carries 400–510 kg CO₂ per tonne from peer-reviewed LCA.
>
> The range reflects pulp grade and allocation treatment of our calcium feedstock.

### ▸ Q3. Has an LCA been performed? (100 / 98)

> Not a third-party LCA. Our figures are internal mass-balance calculations against published pulp footprints, and we state that plainly rather than present them as verified.
>
> System boundary: cradle-to-gate, from quarried and calcined mineral inputs through carbonated product at the plant gate, with displaced pulp handled as an avoided burden.
>
> Major inputs per tonne: roughly 600 kg lime, 3 kg microfibrillated cellulose, 431 kg CO₂, milling and mixing electricity, water. Calcium is the dominant burden and the dominant lever.
>
> Commissioning an independent, ISO-conformant LCA is a specific goal for the next twelve months.

### ▸ Q4. MRV approach (100 / 97)

> Our product is a physical material sold by weight, which makes the primary quantity directly measurable: tonnes shipped under contract, reconciled against invoices.
>
> Carbonate content is verified by TAPPI T211 ash determination, an industry-standard method our customers already run on incoming material, so the mineral fraction is independently confirmed by the buyer rather than asserted by us. Sheet ash content is measured the same way at the mill.
>
> CO₂ consumed is metered at the reactor. Displaced pulp is calculated from furnish records.
>
> We have not yet engaged a third-party verifier. Establishing that protocol is a stated near-term objective.

# SECTION 4: BUSINESS MODEL AND COMMERCIALIZATION (3문항 · 각 200 words)

### ▸ Q1. High-level business model (200 / 189)

> We license technology to filler producers and take a royalty on their sales. We hold no inventory, operate no plant, and carry no working capital against production.
>
> Our customer is the filler producer, not the paper mill. This is worth stating plainly because it is easy to get backwards: **we do not replace filler — we replace pulp.** A mill adopting our product buys the same tonnage of mineral it always did, or more; it buys less wood. The filler category grows rather than shifts, which means incumbent carbonate producers have no volume to defend and every reason to license.
>
> Terms: 5–10% of the producer's sales of our filler, under supply agreements of 10–15 years.
>
> The unit economics are simple. A single mill consumes 5,000–20,000 tonnes annually. At $250–350 per tonne, a representative royalty is around $15 per tonne, so one mill at 10,000 tonnes represents roughly $150,000 in annual royalty to us, against essentially no marginal cost to serve.
>
> The model matches how process technology is normally commercialized in materials industries — the operator owns the plant and the capital, the licensor owns the process. It also means our scaling rate is set by our partners' plant count, not by our balance sheet.

### ▸ Q2. Beachhead market and go-to-market (200 / 195)

> Beachhead: printing and writing grades — copy paper, wallpaper base stock, label stock — supplied through carbonate producers already operating satellite plants at or near mills. This segment runs the highest filler loadings, so the value of raising the ceiling is largest and most immediately legible to a buyer.
>
> Validated: a major pulp and paper producer completed multi-year testing on its own machines and ordered 9,000 tonnes annually. That mill's neighbouring mill has indicated it will adopt if the material performs. A second global filler producer signed an NDA and is running qualification tests.
>
> Go-to-market is deliberately indirect. We license to producers rather than selling to mills, because adoption propagates through the supply chain: once a mill sees the cost saving, it asks its own filler supplier for the same product, and suppliers competing for that account cannot easily decline. One licence reaches every mill that producer serves.
>
> Our current arrangement demonstrates this directly — the order brought our partner a paper mill it had not previously supplied.
>
> We maintain a database of 850 target mills worldwide to sequence expansion by grade, geography, and proximity to concentrated CO₂ sources.

### ▸ Q3. Markets you wish to validate but have not explored (200 / 197)

> **Tissue.** Filler has never been usable in tissue — the sheet is too thin and weak to carry it — so a 40–45 million tonne market has been closed to mineral substitution entirely. Our structure appears to open it. When a competitor attempted to patent flexible carbonate for tissue in Korea, the application was rejected in April 2026 over our CTO's published prior art. Testing moves next to a US university tissue laboratory. We want to learn whether the strength gain holds at tissue basis weights and whether softness is preserved, since softness governs purchase decisions in that category.
>
> **The United States, generally.** We have no US mill data, no US customer, and no US production. We need to learn how US mills evaluate a furnish change, what qualification cycles look like, and whether the cost logic that persuaded an Asian mill translates to a market with different pulp sourcing.
>
> **Non-paper uses of the same material.** Our filings cover composites and stone paper at 50–99% mineral loading, with polymer content roughly half that of existing stone paper. We have laboratory data and no market contact. We want to learn whether packaging or durable-goods buyers value that substitution.

### ▸ Q1a. Key milestone — Technology (100 / 98)

> **Validate our lowest-carbon production route at commercial scale.** Today our filler is made by adding microfibrillated cellulose to an existing carbonate reactor. Our filed data show a better route: co-milling lime, ground carbonate and unrefined pulp together, which halves lime consumption, cuts CO₂ per tonne by roughly 40%, and — in laboratory sheets — produces our highest breaking length and brightness. We hold the chemistry; we need plant-scale process parameters.
>
> Second: secure durable point-source CO₂ supply for production, replacing an arrangement now being renegotiated.

> Third: complete tissue testing at a US laboratory.
